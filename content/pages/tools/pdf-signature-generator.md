## What makes a good signature image

A signature image is used at small sizes, on all sorts of backgrounds, and often printed. That puts a few demands on it which a photo of your signature on paper rarely meets.

- **No background.** A photo includes the paper, and paper is never quite white, so the signature arrives inside a grey or yellow rectangle. A generated signature has a transparent background instead.
- **Tight cropping.** When the image has a lot of empty space, scaling it into a signature box shrinks the actual ink. The generator trims the image to the strokes themselves, with a small margin.
- **Enough resolution.** A signature that looks fine on screen can print as a blur. The generator exports at a size that stays sharp when placed on an A4 or Letter page.
- **Dark ink.** Light colours vanish in greyscale printing and photocopies. Black and dark blue are the safe choices.

## Drawing versus typing

A **drawn** signature is the closest thing to your pen signature. Draw it slowly and at a comfortable size: the stroke is smoothed, so small wobbles disappear, and the line width varies with speed the way ink does. On a phone, turn the screen sideways for more room.

A **typed** signature renders your name in a handwriting typeface. It is consistent, legible and quick, which makes it a good choice for routine documents. It does not look like your pen signature, and it does not need to.

## Keeping your signature image safe

Treat a signature image like a rubber stamp: useful, but not something to leave lying around. Store it somewhere private, do not post it publicly, and do not send it to people as an attachment. For documents where someone might later dispute who signed, do not rely on an image alone. Send the document through a signing workflow that records who opened and signed it; the page on [PDF signatures](/pdf-signature) explains why.
